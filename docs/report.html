AI Foundations final project report

Second Look

An AI second opinion on an investment portfolio that shows what you really own, what it costs, how it would have held up in past crashes and whether you are on track for retirement, with every number computed in Python and only explained by AI.

Second Look uses generative AI and is not a professional financial advisor. Its suggestions are general shifts between industries, asset types and account types, never specific stocks or securities. Sample portfolios and personas are made up.

2. Motivation

Who has the problem. Ordinary investors with a 401(k), an IRA and maybe a brokerage account, who own several funds and a few popular stocks but have never looked inside them. They are not professionals, and many do not pay for an advisor.

How they handle it today. Mostly they don't. Brokerage statements list holdings one account at a time and do not show that three funds own the same companies. Free tools exist but each answers one question (fund overlap, fees or retirement), usually after manual data entry, and general chatbots will happily do arithmetic they can get wrong.

What it costs them. Our sample personas (made up, but built from real tickers and real closing prices) show the kind of thing people miss:

  • Hidden concentration: in the mid-career sample, Apple is 13.3% of the portfolio ($24,068) once fund holdings are counted, while only 7.6% is held directly. Technology is 56.6% of the whole portfolio. (Source: Facts JSON for the mid-career sample)
  • Fees: the retiree sample pays a weighted 0.196% a year; over 30 years at the assumed 6% return, fees cost about $204,147, of which $151,094 is more than index funds would charge. One active fund (GFACX, 1.35%) costs $1,095 a year. (Source: Facts JSON for the retiree sample)
  • Fear: a repeat of the 2008-09 financial crisis would have cut the mid-career portfolio by 54.2% ($98,155) at the worst point. People who sold near the bottom locked that in.
  • How many people this affects: fund ownership is widespread, advice is not. In 2024, 56 percent of US households owned mutual funds or other registered funds (Investment Company Institute, 2025 Fact Book release, April 2025), while only 37% of Americans work with a financial advisor (Northwestern Mutual 2023 Planning & Progress Study, 2,740 US adults). The two surveys measure different groups, so they are not combined into one number here.

Why anyone should care. These are expensive, slow-to-notice mistakes made by people who are trying to do the right thing. A tool that explains them plainly, never sells anything and never picks specific stocks or funds could help without adding a new risk.

3. What is new here

None of the individual analyses is new. Look-through tools, fee calculators, crash backtests and retirement planners all exist. What is different is the combination and the guardrails:

Competitor descriptions are summarized from public pages and reviews, which may change: Morningstar Portfolio X-Ray help, review of Empower's free tools, free ETF overlap tools.

Said honestly: professionals and paid tools go deeper than this (full holdings instead of top 10, tax-lot detail, live account links). Second Look's contribution is a free, explainable, guard-railed second opinion: the AI is used only to explain, a verifier checks every number it writes, and a safe template replaces anything that fails. The newest piece, Try a Shift, closes the loop from analysis to action without risk: after the app suggests shifts, the user can see how a pretend $1,000 would have done in conservative, moderate and risky mixes of asset types, replayed through real market history (2008, Covid, 2022) next to their own portfolio, with the AI explaining the trade-off between growth and drops. Whether that is enough for real users has not been measured: no user study was run, and running one is the first next step (Section 8).

4. The agents

Python does all the finance. The agents never calculate and have no tools to call: they receive the computed Facts JSON and return structured JSON that Python validates. The orchestrator runs analyst → explainer → verifier; on a failure it allows one rewrite with the verifier's reasons, and after a second failure it shows a safe template built straight from the facts. An analysis makes at most 4 AI calls, all through llm.py, which checks the budget before each one.

Why the agents have no tools (a deliberate choice). The tools are real, but they run before the agents, in Python: live and snapshot market data (yfinance), fund look-through, fee projections, crash replays, the retirement plan, the S&P 500 comparison and the Possible-Shifts rules. Their results go to the agents as the Facts JSON, and the verifier's checks (number matcher, advice guard) are tools the orchestrator runs on every draft. Letting a model call calculators itself would make the money math depend on the model's choices, add calls and cost, and give the model actions it does not need. Keeping the agents tool-free means every number can be traced to Python, and nothing an agent writes can trigger an action. In this project, the verifier caught 10 of 10 red-team drafts that contained a wrong number and even the larger model's first drafts passed the verifier only 11% of the time, which is why numbers are computed, not generated.

Which models and why. Both MODEL_FAST and MODEL_SMART are gpt-6-luna through Portkey, the course default at $0.10 / $0.50 per million input / output tokens, with reasoning_effort=low. The larger models are priced in the code (gpt-6-sol $2 / $10, gpt-6-astra $10 / $50) but were not needed: on luna, 78% of first drafts passed the verifier in the quality run (Section 5), and astra's worst case per call exceeds the $0.05 per-call limit.

What was done to cut cost

  • All arithmetic in Python; the AI sees a compact Facts JSON (kept under 2,000 tokens) instead of raw data.
  • The cheapest course model (gpt-6-luna) for every agent; larger models priced but unused.
  • reasoning_effort=low: at the default effort, hidden reasoning sometimes used the whole token allowance and returned nothing, forcing retries and fallbacks.
  • A response cache, a hard cap of 4 AI calls per analysis, and budget limits per call ($0.05), per session ($0.10) and per day ($2.00), all enforced in budget.py before each call.
  • Demo mode replays recorded answers for $0.

6. Safety

Safety policy. Second Look explains numbers and, in its Possible Shifts tab, suggests general shifts between industries, asset types (stocks, bonds, cash, low-cost index funds) and account types. Those suggestions come from fixed Python rules applied to the user's numbers, and the AI only words them. It never recommends a specific stock, fund or other security, never sets price targets, never predicts markets and never promises returns; the Top 3 explanations and the Pause Button stay neutral. Every page carries a disclaimer that this is generative AI, not a professional financial advisor. Every number shown is computed in Python; the AI may only explain numbers it is given, and a verifier rejects any draft with a number that is not in the facts, advice-like wording, or a retirement figure that is not a labeled range. Uploaded files are processed in memory, account numbers and free text are removed before anything reaches the AI, and the API key lives only in .env, which git never tracks. When anything fails, users see a safe summary built straight from the numbers.

Threats and defenses

8. Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • Fund look-through uses only each fund's top ~10 holdings, so company exposures are minimums.
  • Prices come from Yahoo Finance (unofficial) with an offline snapshot through 2026-10-16; sample portfolios always use the snapshot.
  • The retirement plan is simple: constant real returns, a flat 15% tax on pre-tax accounts and no other taxes.
  • The glide-path reference and the risk-profile ranges are placeholders until a cited source is added.
  • The stricter plan-range rule makes the model verifier send more first drafts back for a rewrite. Its reasons are now recorded on the Admin page, but the rule has not been tuned yet.
  • Demo mode has saved answers only for the three samples with their default answers; uploads get the standard summary.
  • No user study has been run; the app is not deployed online; the macOS setup is untested.

With another month

  • Run a user study (5-6 people, timed against a spreadsheet, with a comprehension check).
  • Full fund holdings from fund filings instead of top 10.
  • Tune the verifier using its recorded reasons to cut rewrites.
  • Deploy online with proper secret handling.
  • Monte Carlo ranges instead of three fixed scenarios; basic tax awareness.
  • Parking lot (raised during the project, deliberately set aside): AI-chosen shifts (kept as fixed Python rules the verifier can check); suggestions in Top 3 and the Pause Button (kept neutral); every agent on the larger model (24x the cost, fewer drafts passing); a user-chosen amount in Try a Shift (kept at $1,000 so demo answers stay valid); cross-checking costs against Portkey's logs (the course key cannot export them).
